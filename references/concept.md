# Concept before keyframes

A promo that is paced well but built sentence by sentence reads as polished slides: each line
gets a picture, nothing carries across, and a viewer cannot say afterwards what the film was.
Fix the concept before any keyframe. It takes minutes and decides every shot after it.

Write three things into `brief.md` under `## Concept`.

## 1. One-line concept

What the film is, in one sentence. Not a summary of the narration.

- Narration: "I kept every failed sketch for ten years. This course is what they taught me."
- Summary (not a concept): "The teacher presents her new drawing course."
- Concept: "A drawer of failed sketches that refills itself: every lesson pulls one out and
  finishes it."

The concept names a picture you can build, not a message you hope the viewer infers.

## 2. Organizing device

One visible line that runs through the whole film, moves forward, and flips when it tops out.
Forms that work:

- a countdown (10 → 0, then something happens at zero);
- a numbered sequence (take 1, take 2; page 1 … page 240; step N of N);
- a progress bar or a fill level;
- station names, a route, a map pin moving;
- a year scale (1998 → 2026);
- chapter or section labels.

Rules:

- It grows out of the content. "Ten years of sketches" suggests a sketch count; a book suggests
  page numbers; a course suggests lesson numbers. Do not bolt on a timer because timers
  are dramatic.
- It is visible in the picture: on a sign, a page corner, a screen, a label on an object, a
  lower-third counter. Words in the subtitle alone do not count.
- It changes state at least every few bars and reaches its top near the turn.
- At the top it flips: the count reaches zero and the drawer comes out empty; the page number
  passes the last page and turns into a blank page for the viewer; the progress bar fills and
  becomes the book's spine.

## 3. Closing prop

One concrete object at the end that states the film's claim for it. It is held long enough to
read (1.5–3 s) and belongs only to this subject. Examples: the finished drawing pinned over the
first failed sketch; a certificate signed on the last lesson; a receipt that lists
what the viewer gets. The real asset may be the prop, or appear beside it.

## Self-check (three questions)

Answer each in one line in `brief.md`. If an answer fails, rethink before any keyframe.

1. **Swap test.** Put a different narration under this device. Does it still work? If yes, the
   device is too generic. Make it depend on something only this content has.
2. **Screenshot test.** Which single frame would a stranger screenshot and send to a friend?
   Name it (time and what is in it). If you cannot name one, the film does not have it yet.
3. **First-frame test.** Does the first frame belong only to this subject? Same rule as
   [cinematic.md](cinematic.md#the-first-two-seconds): if it could open any other video, redo it.

## Then the scene list

With the concept fixed, write `## Scene list` in `brief.md` (format in
[SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#author-and-deliver), density rules in
[directing.md](directing.md#density-a-new-idea-every-bar)). Every row names the device's state,
so a reader can follow the device from first row to last and see where it flips.

## What this is not

- Not a script rewrite. A finalized narration stays as supplied.
- Not a license to invent facts. The device and the prop are staging; they do not add numbers,
  dates or claims that the source does not make. A year scale uses years the source states.
- Not a second approval step. Write it, check it, proceed.
